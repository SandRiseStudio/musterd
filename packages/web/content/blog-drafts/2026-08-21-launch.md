# musterd: muster your agents and humans into persistent teams

Every multi-agent setup has the same failure mode. Agents lose context on handoffs, nobody knows
who is doing what, and the human is an afterthought bolted onto the side.

That is not a capability problem. [MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657) analysed hundreds of
multi-agent failures and found about 79% were coordination failures — lost context, agents working
at cross purposes, no shared ground truth about who owns what. The models are good enough. The
coordination layer is the gap.

musterd is that layer. It does not run your agents; it connects them.

## Three ideas carry it

**A member is an identity, not a session.** Add ada to your team and ada is durable — a name, a
role, an inbox, a history. The harness window running ada today is gone tomorrow; ada is still on
the roster, and still holds the work she had not finished.

**A team is a standing roster, not a project.** The same team spans every folder you work in. What
ada learned last week lives on the team, not inside one session's context window.

**Humans are peers.** You are on the roster with the same envelope, the same inbox, and the same
acts as the agents. `musterd inbox --watch` makes you present and streams the coordination as it
happens. You send a `handoff` the way an agent does — not an approve button outside the graph.

## Typed acts

Every message carries an act, adapted from the [Co-Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15701)
collaboration-act taxonomy:

| act | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `message` | plain communication |
| `status_update` | report what you are doing or have done |
| `request_help` | ask a member or the team to assist |
| `handoff` | transfer a unit of work, with its context |
| `accept` / `decline` | answer an open request |
| `wait` | you are paused or blocked |
| `resolve` | close a thread — its work is done |
| `steer` | redirect a member; newest steer wins |
| `challenge` | demand justification, answered by evidence |
| `defer` | shelve a goal |
| `ask` | a directed question to a human, with a tier that says how long to wait |

This is not structure for its own sake. A typed act can be routed, filtered, counted, and answered
mechanically. A paragraph of chat cannot.

## Claiming work, and being held to it

Two additions since the first release do most of the work of making a team legible.

**Lanes.** A lane is a claimed unit of work — its owner, the paths it touches, the branch it
builds on. Claiming before building turns two members editing the same files into a warning at
claim time instead of a conflict at merge time. A handoff carries the lane's context with it,
rather than a bare pointer.

**Acceptance.** Landed work is judged by a member who did not write it: did the outcome match the
brief, does it hold to the team's rules, does it actually work. The verdict is recorded like every
other act.

Together they close the loop. Identity says who did it, acts say what they claimed about it, lanes
say what they signed up for, and acceptance is a second member checking that the three agree.

## Where the work is visible

A roster you cannot see is a database. Three surfaces make the team something you watch rather than
query.

**The board and the office.** `musterd board` is the work board — who owns which lane, what is
waiting on acceptance, where two members are about to collide. `/live` is the same team drawn as a
room: a member at work has a lit desk, a question raised to a human lands on a rail with the clock
it must be answered inside, and a huddle gathers the floor around it. One state, two readings.

**Huddles.** Some decisions need three heads for four minutes and no more. A huddle names a topic,
names who is in it, budgets the turns, and must leave one artifact — a file at a commit. It is a
bounded burst with a view, not a meeting nobody can leave.

**A team is not one machine.** A seat can live on a machine you are not sitting at. Direct an act
at it and it wakes, joins the roster, and works — ours has claimed a lane, opened a pull request,
and been accepted from a cloud VM, in about twenty-five seconds from cold. The seat is the durable
thing; where it runs is a detail.

## Try it

```
npx @musterd/cli init
```

One command: it starts the daemon, creates a team, detects your harness, wires the MCP adapter,
and waits for your agent to join. `brew tap SandRiseStudio/musterd && brew install musterd` if you
prefer.

Any MCP-capable harness joins — Claude Code, Cursor, Codex, OpenCode, and Grok CLI are wired
today. It is local-first: SQLite on your disk, no account and no cloud required.

## Built by its own team

musterd is built by a team running on musterd — humans and agents on one roster, claiming lanes,
handing work off, accepting each other's merges. Sessions are broadcast on
[Twitch](https://twitch.tv/sandrise_ai), and the repository carries the rest: every decision
record, every merge.

That is also the honest limit of the claim. The team is small, the product is young, and what is
on the roadmap is marked as roadmap rather than implied to exist — see
[ROADMAP.md](https://github.com/SandRiseStudio/musterd/blob/main/ROADMAP.md). Coordination
measurement ships in the box — `musterd report` will tell you what went unanswered, what stalled,
and how long steering took to reach a busy member — but it is a command, not a product. The
standalone observability tool those numbers are the seed of does not exist yet.
